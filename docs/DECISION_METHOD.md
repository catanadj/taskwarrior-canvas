# Kyanbasu Decision Method

This is the decision process Kyanbasu should support. It combines Annie Duke's treatment of uncertainty and counterfactuals with Stephen Duneier's outcome–states–acts model and emphasis on advance planning. The method is useful without any special Canvas functionality; the Canvas should make it easier to follow, not replace judgment.

## Use the right amount of process

Spend more effort when a decision is consequential, difficult to reverse, or expensive to correct. For a low-impact or easily reversible decision, use only the parts that help and decide quickly.

## The decision record

A decision record distinguishes these elements:

- **Target:** the desired outcome and the criteria by which success will be recognized.
- **Factor:** something that affects the target but is not directly controlled. This includes external conditions, constraints, and personal habits that are unlikely to change merely because a plan requires it.
- **Option:** an act that can be chosen and performed.
- **Possible outcome:** a result that could follow from an option.
- **Signal:** a factor with genuine predictive or decision-changing value.
- **Trigger:** an observable event or date paired in advance with a response.

## Process

### 1. Frame the decision

Describe the problem neutrally rather than beginning with a preferred solution or a binary “should I?” question. Record:

- What outcome am I trying to achieve?
- What would success look like?
- When must I decide?
- How costly or reversible is the decision?

### 2. Record the present state of knowledge

Write down what is known, what is assumed, and what remains unknown before the result can influence memory. Seek the outside view first: relevant base rates, comparable cases, and perspectives that do not already share the preferred conclusion. Then add the details particular to this situation.

Stop gathering information when no affordable information is likely to change the choice.

### 3. Identify factors and signals

List the conditions that affect success but are not directly controlled. Treat persistent habits, available attention, schedule, energy, and other practical limitations as real factors rather than assuming they will disappear.

Identify which factors would actually change the forecast or choice. These are signals; the rest should not drive reactive decisions.

### 4. Generate options

Record more than one genuine act when possible, including doing nothing, waiting, or running a small reversible experiment. Options describe controllable actions, not hoped-for results.

### 5. Explore possible outcomes

For each serious option, identify the reasonable outcomes that could occur—not only the desired or most vivid one. For each outcome, estimate:

- A best-guess probability and, when useful, a reasonable lower and upper bound.
- The payoff or cost in the values that matter: time, health, money, relationships, learning, flexibility, or another relevant measure.
- Which factors or assumptions most influence that estimate.

Probabilities express uncertainty; they are not promises. A range should widen when knowledge is weak.

For important options, trace consequences beyond the immediate result:

1. **First order:** What happens directly if this option is taken?
2. **Second order:** What is likely to follow from each important first-order consequence?
3. **Third order:** What longer-range or systemic effect could follow after that?

Record multiple plausible consequences at any order when the future can branch. For each one, note its time horizon, likelihood, payoff or cost, and the value affected. The purpose is to expose delayed tradeoffs and branching uncertainty—not to force a single linear prediction or calculate an automatic score.

### 6. Stress-test the plan

Imagine that the option failed and work backward to plausible causes. Then imagine that it succeeded and identify what enabled it. Separate causes within control from states or luck outside control. Use the result to improve the option, add a hedge, or reject it.

An inversion pass makes the failure side explicit:

1. State the opposite of the desired outcome.
2. Ask how that opposite outcome could be deliberately produced.
3. Record the resulting failure modes and their early-warning signals.
4. Attach a prevention, detection, or response safeguard to each important failure mode.

Inversion can be applied to the whole decision or to one option. Its purpose is to expose avoidable failure before commitment, not to prove that every risk can be removed.

### 7. Choose and precommit

Choose the option with the best overall case, not the option attached to the most attractive single outcome. Preserve every unchosen branch as a counterfactual; an unchosen option is not proven wrong.

Before acting, record:

- Why this option was selected.
- What information would challenge the rationale.
- A time-based reassessment point.
- Observable triggers and the response to each one.
- Conditions for stopping, changing course, or taking a hedge.

Commit a snapshot of the record as it existed at the time of choice.

### 8. Execute the process

Translate the chosen option and its protections into actions that fit the actual states. When an action is difficult to start, reduce it to a small executable step—often five to ten minutes—rather than relying on a sudden change in motivation or habits.

Follow the precommitted plan when its trigger occurs. Depart from it only for a clear reason, and record that reason.

### 9. Review without resulting

After enough time has passed, record the actual outcome without deleting the other possible outcomes. Review the quality of the process separately from the quality of the result:

- Was the framing clear and neutral?
- Were important options, factors, and outcomes omitted?
- Were the probability estimates reasonable given what was known then?
- Was evidence mistaken for noise, or noise for signal?
- Were the precommitments followed?
- What was skill or process, and what was luck?
- What should change in the next decision?

A good outcome does not prove that the decision was good, and a bad outcome does not prove that it was poor. The purpose of review is to improve the next process, not rewrite the previous one with hindsight.

## Compact checklist

For an ordinary personal decision, the minimum useful record is:

1. Target and success criteria.
2. Important factors, including actual personal constraints.
3. At least two options.
4. Reasonable possible outcomes for each serious option.
5. Rough likelihoods and meaningful payoffs.
6. Chosen option and choice-time snapshot.
7. At least one reassessment date or signal-and-response trigger.
8. Later process review kept separate from the result.
